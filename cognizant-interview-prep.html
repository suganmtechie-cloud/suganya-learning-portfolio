03 · Design and facilitate

How to answer a training-design question

Use this repeatable chain for “How would you design a behavioural programme?” or “How do you make learning effective?”

1 · DiagnoseBusiness need, audience, behaviour gap
→
2 · DefineObservable learning objectives
→
3 · DesignContent, scenarios, practice
→
4 · FacilitateRole-play, discussion, feedback
→
5 · EvaluateLearning, behaviour, results

Example: communication

Need: freshers struggle to communicate clearly in team discussions. Objectives: structure a concise update, listen actively and respond constructively.

Practice activities

Demonstration of a good/bad conversation, paired role-play, group feedback, reflection and a repeat attempt using an observation checklist.

Follow-through

Pre/post knowledge check, facilitator rubric, learner reflection and manager observation after training. Track application, not just satisfaction.

Adult learning: what should I remember?
Adults want relevance, respect and application. Connect content to their work, build on prior experience, let them practise real tasks, give choice where possible and explain why the learning matters. Avoid long lectures without interaction.
Experiential learning: explain simply
Learners move through experience → reflection → concept → experimentation. Example: role-play a difficult conversation, reflect on what happened, introduce a listening framework, then repeat the conversation with a better approach.
How would you handle different learner levels?
Check prior knowledge first. Give beginners a clear foundation, examples and guided practice. Give experienced learners complex cases, problem-solving and peer discussion. Use short knowledge checks to adjust pace and support.

Measurement formulas

Know the basics

  • Completion % = completed ÷ enrolled × 100
  • Pass rate % = passed ÷ assessed × 100
  • Certification % = certified ÷ eligible × 100
  • Cost per learner = total training cost ÷ learners
  • ROI % = (monetary benefits − training cost) ÷ training cost × 100

Also mention time to proficiency, engagement, skill demonstration and workplace application.

Learning design concepts

One-line recall

  • Bloom: remember → understand → apply → analyse → evaluate → create.
  • Kolb: experience, reflect, conceptualise, experiment.
  • Microlearning: short, focused learning around one objective.
  • Blended learning: combine live facilitation and self-paced practice.
  • Psychological safety: learners can ask, practise and make mistakes without humiliation.
05 · Scenario practice

Answer with a calm, repeatable structure

For each answer: diagnose first, take a practical action, then explain how you will follow up or measure impact.

A participant is aggressive or dominates the session. What do you do?
Stay calm and avoid personalising it. Acknowledge the concern, restate the session purpose and set respectful boundaries. Invite the person to explain the specific issue, then redirect to the topic and make space for others. If needed, discuss it privately after the session. Measure: participation balance and whether the group can continue learning.
Training scores are high, but workplace performance has not improved.
Do not assume the course worked. Check whether the assessment measured the right skill, whether learners had opportunities to practise, whether managers reinforced the behaviour and whether workplace barriers exist. Gather learner and manager feedback, observe application, revise the learning intervention and measure again. Connect to Kirkpatrick Behaviour and Results.
A manager says training is a waste of time.
First understand the business concern and the performance problem. Agree what improvement would matter, then show relevant evidence—skill gaps, learner assessment, application and business indicators. Offer a small targeted pilot rather than defending training in general. Speak in outcomes, not attendance.
You need to train 500 freshers across locations.
Segment into cohorts, define common outcomes and standard facilitator materials, use blended delivery for scale, centralise resources and assessments in the LMS, train facilitators, collect cohort-level data and run quality checks. Use common rubrics and calibration to keep delivery consistent while allowing local support.
How would you coach an underperforming learner?
Use GROW. Agree a specific goal, understand the current reality without judgement, explore options and practice, then agree a measurable action and review date. Give specific feedback using SBI. Coaching is supportive and accountable—not simply telling someone what to do.
What if you have not led a large behavioural leadership programme?
Be transparent: “I have not independently led a large-scale leadership programme. My strongest experience is technical enablement and learning operations, with behavioural exposure through facilitation and learner engagement. I understand the design principles and can contribute while building deeper expertise in advanced coaching and leadership interventions.” Then give a relevant real example.
06 · AI-enabled learning

Show curiosity and responsible judgement

You do not need to sound like an AI engineer. Explain a practical learning use case and its safeguards.

Use case

AI role-play coach

A learner practises a workplace conversation with an AI simulation—for example, responding to a customer concern or giving feedback to a colleague. The tool can offer structured feedback on clarity, empathy, listening and professionalism, then suggest a second attempt.

Human oversight

Safeguards matter

  • Protect personal and confidential information.
  • Check for bias and inaccurate or hallucinated feedback.
  • Use transparent rubrics and trainer review.
  • Do not let AI make high-stakes people decisions.
  • Keep human coaching and reflection in the loop.
“I see AI as a way to increase practice opportunities and provide timely feedback—not as a replacement for human facilitation, empathy or trainer judgement.”

What does “Full Stack Educator” mean? Should I ask?
